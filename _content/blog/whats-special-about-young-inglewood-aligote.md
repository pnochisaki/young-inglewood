While in terms of land use we chose the most economical spotpossible on which to build our winery, we still had to take out about twothirds of an acre of Cabernet Sauvignon vines. To make up for that, we determined to plant new vines as soon aspossible, next to the winery in whatever area was left after it was complete.

 

Since the vines were to be new, we wanted to plant a newvariety - one we’re not already growing. We always assumed it would be a red variety, then  somewhere along the line, the idea of plantinga white variety took root as it were.    Backin the late 1800s when grapevines were first planted in our neighborhood, theywere almost exclusively white varieties, most seldom seen here now.  So we thought we’d recreate a littleneighborhood history and plant white grapes.

 

But which variety? Anyone who knows us knows we love white Burgundy, made from Chardonnay.  We model Young Inglewood Chardonnays afterthe best examples of white Burgundy. Knowing that the Oak Knoll vineyard where we grow our Chardonnay isbetter suited climatically to producing that grape, we looked for a different whitevariety.

 

Something Different

 

The primary white grape of Burgundy is Chardonnay. The Burgundians however grow another lesser known, but widely respected white winevariety:  Aligoté.  Moreover, the wines produced from it are delightful– crisp and delicious.  Most of the majorproducers of white Burgundy produce their own version of Aligoté in addition totheir Chardonnays.  As a furtherhistorical note, Aligoté used to be widely planted in California but is nowconfined to less than a handful of producers. Able to pay our respects to the history of both Napa Valley andBurgundy, our choice was made: Aligoté.  It would be fun andexciting to see the character of the grape we could grow in our corner of theNapa Valley.

 

How and when to plant.

 

First, a little wine history. Grapevines grown for wine are an ancient species called Vitis vinifera,native to Europe.  In the late 1800s, thethriving European wine industry which depended on it was devastated by a peskylouse called phylloxera that ate its way through vinifera roots and killed thevines, (causing the ominous epithet “vastatatrix” to be appended to  the louse’s name).  Due to the import of vinifera for theburgeoning American wine industry, the louse migrated to the United States,devastating vineyards here too.  Fortunately,it was discovered that native American grapevines, while not suitable forproducing wine, were resistant to phylloxera.   It was further discovered that viniferacuttings, grafted to the rootstocks of native American vines, would grow into viniferagrapevines.  Since the 1800s therefore,it has been common practice for vineyards throughout the world to grow wine inthis way.

 

How is this done?

 

The two most successful ways are by field budding and bench grafts.  In the first, vinifera is grafted on toalready planted rootstocks.  In thesecond the grafting is done in the nursery and the plants are babied along abit before they are planted in the vineyard. Our preference was to use bench grafts to give the vines a littlematurity before we planted them.  

 

Next hurdle – how to find the vines?

 

Since Aligoté is rarely planted any more in the US it was achallenge to find a source, still more one we could rely on for healthyplants.  Enter our vineyard manager DougHill, who planted our vineyard.  Douglocated the Aligoté through Foundation Plant Services, a department of UC Davisthat produces and distributes virus & disease-tested plants.  Doug also contracted with a nursery to graftthe vines onto our chosen rootstocks. While there weren’t quite enough plants to cover the area we hadinitially planned, we felt it was better to plant fewer vines than to fill theremaining area with rootstocks and wait another year before we could graftAligoté vines on to them.

 

Preparing to Plant

 

Doug located the vines in March 2013 and we were able toplant them just before the 2013 harvest. In the intervening time there was much to do.

 

After preparing the soil for planting, the next task was topick the spacing between the vines and install a trellising system – the systemof poles and wires that support the vines as they grow. We decided to followthe Burgundian tradition, as well as the tradition in our existing vineyard andchoose the Guyot trellising system, named for Jules Guyot, an early 19thcentury French agronomist, known for improvements in the cultivation of grapevines for fine wine.  In the Guyotsystem, vines can be trained to grow either from a single “cordon”, or twocordons.  Since Aligoté is a vigorousgrower, we picked the double cordon or “double Guyot”, which forces the vine todivide its energy.  We installed thenecessary poles and wires, which created our vine rows.

 

Water

 

We needed a water supply to help the young vines thrive. Forthis a system of pipes connected to our vineyard well was buried in the groundwith above ground connections at the end of each row of grapes.  From each above ground connection a hose wasrun along the trellis wire.  At each vineposition, a thin piece of hose was trained vertically downwards so that watercould trickle into the root zone of each plant when the hoses were turned on.  This system is known as drip irrigation.  It is more water efficient and more effectivethan sprinkler systems because the water only gets to where it is needed – theroots.  If the leaves get wet, they canbe burned by the sun as they dry out and the vine loses an important source offood.

 

Sprinklers, however, are highly effective for protectingvines from the frosts that can occur here. The sprinkler water warms up the vines and stops them from freezing inthe event of frost that can occur early in the growing season.  A frozen vine is a dead vine!  With this in mind, a sprinkler, lookingexactly like the sprinklers you find in a home for fire protection, was addedto the top of each vine pole.  

 

Planted.

 

Once the 2013 harvest season was over, and the vines indormancy, the Aligoté grafts were put in the ground at last with the expertadvice of Steve Matthiasson our viticultural advisor. We pruned the plantshoots in March 2014, along with all our other estate vines. By the middle of April,we had enough growth to be able to select the strongest shoots destined tobecome the vine trunks and pinch off the others.  By the end of May, we were able to select the“double Guyot”, the two shoots per vine chosen to become canes and thencordons,  the “arms” of the vine, from whichcanes that eventually produce grapes grow. 

 

What was our first vintage?

 

Astonishingly, we made a tiny amount of Aligoté in 2015, justone year later! At harvest time our small team picked the ripe Aligoté grapesourselves. There weren’t enough for us to use our mechanical press. Instead, wepressed the juice from the grapes by hand through a sieve. It took severalhours but our labors resulted in 10 cases of wine – a pretty respectable amountunder the circumstances! 

 

Young Inglewood Aligoté then and now

 

The juice from our first couple of vintages was 100% aged instainless steel barrels.  The finishedwine had distinct tropical fruit flavors – pineapple, melon and mango. After thosefirst few vintages, we felt the grapes were mature enough to be introduced to neutralFrench oak barrels. In the 2018(?) vintage, we had enough grapes to produce aside by side comparison between stainless steel and oak aged wines. (We used stickersdepicting barrels to tell the bottles apart!) We found that we preferred thenuance and complexity introduced by the oak, and we have aged our Aligoté in gentlyused oak barrels ever since.

 

What’s the current vintage and how does it taste?

 

Whenever I drink Young Inglewood Aligoté, no matter what theseason, the wine transports me instantly to a mild Spring day, sunny and nottoo hot, with a gentle breeze. As refreshing as a plunge in a pool on a hotday, it’s lightly perfumed with citrus blossoms and a touch of spice on thefinish.
